When people talk about "the deposit", they usually mean one number. In practice, buying a home involves two different deposits, and mixing them up is one of the most common surprises for first home buyers.

- **The bank deposit** is the share of the purchase price you contribute yourself. It decides how much you can borrow.
- **The seller deposit** is a payment you make to the seller once your offer is locked in. It shows you're committed to the purchase.

They often come from the same money, but they are paid at different times, to different people, for different reasons.

## What is the bank deposit?

The bank deposit is your own contribution towards the home. If you buy a $700,000 home with a 10% deposit, you put in $70,000 and the bank lends the remaining $630,000.

The size of this deposit affects which loans you can get and how much you can borrow. It can come from a mix of sources, such as savings, a KiwiSaver first home withdrawal, or help from family. Our guide to [where your deposit can come from](/first-home-playbook/deposit-sources) covers each option.

Most of the bank deposit is paid on **settlement day**, the day the home becomes yours.

## What is the seller deposit?

The seller deposit is a payment made earlier, once your offer becomes unconditional (all your conditions, like finance and the building report, have been met). At an auction, it's usually paid on the day.

It's commonly around 10% of the price, but the amount is set in the sale and purchase agreement, so it can be negotiated. It's normally paid into the real estate agent's trust account, rather than straight to the seller.

The seller deposit is not an extra cost. It counts towards the purchase price, so whatever you pay at this stage is taken off what's due at settlement.

## Why the difference matters

The timing is where the two deposits catch people out.

A KiwiSaver first home withdrawal can be used for the seller deposit, but only if it's planned for. The withdrawal can take 10 to 15 working days to process, the sale and purchase agreement needs a clause covering it, and the money is held until settlement rather than passed to the seller. It also can't be used for the deposit at an auction. Our article on [using KiwiSaver for your first home](/articles/kiwisaver-first-home-withdrawal) explains how this works.

Cash, on the other hand, can be paid straight away and released to the seller sooner. A recent first home buyer we worked with found this out while making an offer. Their deposit was a mix of KiwiSaver and cash savings, and the agent suggested using the cash as the seller deposit because the owners needed funds for their next purchase, while the KiwiSaver money would go towards settlement.

## Can the seller deposit help your offer?

It can. Some sellers need their deposit early, for example to pay the deposit on their own next home. A buyer who can pay a solid seller deposit promptly can make their offer more appealing, even if the price is the same as another offer.

On the other hand, if your available cash is limited, you can ask for a smaller seller deposit. Many sellers will accept this, especially when the rest of the offer is strong.

## How to plan for both

1. **Know your total deposit and where each part is coming from.** List your cash, KiwiSaver and any family help separately.
2. **Work out how much cash you can pay before settlement.** This is the realistic ceiling for your seller deposit.
3. **Check the deposit amount and timing before you sign.** Your lawyer can review this in the sale and purchase agreement, add a KiwiSaver clause if you need one, and suggest other changes.
4. **Talk to your adviser early.** They can confirm how much the bank needs from you and make sure the timing of each payment works.

Once you go unconditional, you're committed to the purchase, and backing out can mean losing your seller deposit and more. Getting the numbers clear before you make an offer avoids that pressure.

> **The bottom line:** the bank deposit decides how much you can borrow, and the seller deposit secures the home. Plan for both, and make sure you have enough cash available before settlement to cover the seller deposit.
