A LIM and a building report answer two different questions. A **LIM (Land Information Memorandum)** tells you what the local council knows about the property and the land, such as consents, zoning, drainage, rates and natural hazards. A **building report** tells you what condition the house is actually in, based on an inspector walking through it.

You usually want both, because each one covers the other's blind spots. And while a vendor's report can be a useful starting point, it's generally not something to rely on by itself. Here's why.

## What is a LIM?

A LIM is a report from the city or district council that pulls together all the information the council holds about a property. It typically includes:

- **building consents, permits and code compliance certificates (CCCs)**, plus any notices or orders on the property
- **district plan zoning**, which affects what you can build or change
- **stormwater and sewer** drainage information
- **rates** owing or charged
- **natural hazards**, such as flooding, erosion, landslip or liquefaction
- **contamination** or hazardous substances the council knows about

Councils must provide a LIM within 10 working days of the request, and many offer a faster option for an extra fee. The cost varies by council, but is usually several hundred dollars. Your lawyer can order it for you, or you can order it directly from the council's website.

## What has changed with LIMs?

LIMs now present natural hazard information more clearly. Since late 2025, councils must include a separate natural hazards section, covering known hazards, how climate change may make them worse, and information passed on from the regional council.

This makes hazards easier to spot, which matters because they can affect your insurance and, in some cases, your lending. If a LIM shows a hazard, talk to your insurer and your adviser before you go unconditional.

## What doesn't a LIM tell you?

A LIM only shows what the council knows. It won't tell you:

- **the physical condition of the house**, such as leaks, rot or wiring problems
- **about work done without consent**, if the council has no record of it

That second point is important. If a deck, bathroom or extra bedroom isn't mentioned in the LIM, it may have been built without consent. Compare the LIM with what you see at the property, and raise anything that doesn't match with your lawyer.

## What is a building report?

A building report (also called a pre-purchase inspection) is an independent check of the property's condition by a building inspector. A standard inspection follows the New Zealand standard **NZS 4306:2005 Residential Property Inspection**, and looks for issues like:

- moisture, damp and signs of leaks
- rot, borer and structural damage
- roof, cladding and drainage problems
- obvious plumbing or electrical concerns

Most inspections are **visual and non invasive**, so the inspector can't see behind walls or under finished floors. If they suspect moisture or a leaky home, they may recommend invasive testing or a specialist report. Costs vary with the size and type of property, but generally run from several hundred dollars to around a thousand.

A building report also won't confirm whether work has consent. That's what the LIM and council records are for, which is why the two reports work best together.

## Should you trust the vendor's report?

Sellers sometimes provide a LIM or building report upfront, especially for auctions and deadline sales. These can help you decide whether a property is worth pursuing, but there are good reasons not to rely on them alone.

- **You weren't the client.** The report was prepared for the seller (or the agent). If it misses something, you usually have no legal recourse against the inspector or council, because they had no contract with you.
- **It may be out of date.** A LIM is only accurate as at the date it was issued, and a building report reflects the house on the day of the inspection. Check the dates and ask whether any work has been done since.
- **Check the scope.** Look at what the report excludes. Some don't cover weathertightness, roof spaces or subfloors.
- **Check who wrote it.** Look for an inspector who works to NZS 4306, holds professional indemnity insurance, and belongs to a recognised body such as Building Officials Institute of New Zealand (BOINZ) or the New Zealand Institute of Building Inspectors.

Some inspectors will readdress a vendor's report to you for a fee, which can give you the same protection as commissioning it yourself. Even so, many buyers choose their own inspector, so the report is written with their interests in mind.

Real estate agents are expected to recommend that buyers get their own report, and they must tell you if they know a previous report found problems, even if those problems were later fixed.

## When should you get them?

For a negotiated sale, make your offer conditional on a satisfactory LIM and building report, so you can walk away or renegotiate if something serious comes up. Conditions are commonly set at 10 working days, and the [first home journey](/first-home-playbook/journey) shows where each step sits in the process.

For an auction, or any unconditional offer, all your checks need to be done **before** you bid or sign. That means ordering the LIM and building report earlier, and accepting you may pay for reports on a property you don't end up buying.

## How to use both reports

1. **Read the LIM with your lawyer.** Ask them to flag anything unusual, such as missing CCCs, hazards or outstanding notices.
2. **Walk the property with the LIM in hand.** Check that additions and renovations line up with the consents listed.
3. **Read the full building report, not just the summary.** Ask the inspector to explain anything you don't understand.
4. **Tell your insurer and adviser about any hazards or major issues** before your conditions are due.

Budget for both reports early. Our guide to [the hidden costs of buying](/first-home-playbook/costs-of-buying) covers these and other upfront costs.

> **The bottom line:** a LIM tells you what the council knows, and a building report tells you what condition the house is in. Use both, treat a vendor's report as a starting point rather than a guarantee, and have your lawyer review anything that raises questions before you commit.
